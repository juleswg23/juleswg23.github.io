Tables Don’t Have to Be Boring

Who are you listening to?

  • gt-extras author, Great Tables contributor

I want to quickly introduce myself. I’m Jules, and I’m the author of gt-extras and a contributor to Great Tables. Throughout my life, I’ve been drawn to patterns and finding ways to help others see patterns in data. I play sports and games—table tennis, soccer, chess, climbing—and I especially love logic puzzles like KenKen. And this is the thing: tables are like puzzles too. They’re a great place to see patterns if they’re designed well.

The Problem

“They say a picture is worth a thousand words, so why do we have to look at tables with a thousand cells of text?”

Here’s what most people believe: “Charts tell the real stories. Tables are just data dumps.”

I disagree.

Everyone assumes tables are boring. That charts are where the real storytelling happens. But here’s the thing: tables can be just as powerful, if not more so. They can be beautiful, engaging, and tell stories that charts sometimes can’t. In this talk, I’m going to show you why—and more importantly, I’m going to show you that you have the tools to create amazing tables right now.

What Makes It Work?

  • Strategic color (green/yellow/red for performance)
  • Embedded visualizations (win/loss, dumbbells)
  • Clear hierarchy and organization
  • Professional polish

Every choice here serves the data. Nothing is decorative. The color choices make performance visible immediately. The embedded visualizations let you see patterns across multiple dimensions—wins, losses, streaks, scoring trends—all at once. The hierarchy guides your eye to what matters. And the polish signals professionalism. This table isn’t just informative; it’s beautiful. And that beauty isn’t frivolous—it makes the data more accessible and more memorable.

This Wasn’t Hand-Coded HTML

Python + Great Tables + gt-extras

No web design degree required.

This NFL table wasn’t built by manually writing HTML and CSS. It was built entirely in Python, using Great Tables for the structure and formatting, and gt-extras for the visualizations and theming. The code is clean and chainable—each method adds a new layer to the story. And importantly, you don’t need to be a designer or web developer to make something like this. The tools do the heavy lifting.

The Components of a Table

  • Table Header (optional)
  • Stub & Stub Head (optional)
  • Column Labels (required)
  • Table Body (required)
  • Table Footer (optional)

[INSERT: Great Tables component diagram image here]

This is the key insight: tables have structure. They’re not monoliths. They’re composed of distinct parts, each serving a purpose. There’s a header at the top where you put your title and subtitle. There’s the stub on the left where you put row labels and group them. There are column headers, optionally with spanners that group related columns. There’s the body where the data lives. And there’s a footer where you put source notes and footnotes. Once you see tables this way, you realize you can be intentional about each part. You can design each part to support your story.

Understanding the Building Blocks

Part 1: What is Great Tables?

Great Tables Foundation

The Components of a Table

  • Created by Rich Iannone (originally gt in R)
  • Now available in Python (Michael Chow at Posit)
  • Philosophy: Tables are a first-class visualization medium

In other words: tables deserve to be designed.

Great Tables started with Rich Iannone’s work on the R package called gt. Michael Chow at Posit then ported it to Python, and now we have a powerful tool for building beautiful, publication-ready tables. The philosophy is simple: tables aren’t second-class citizens in data visualization. They’re a powerful medium in their own right. And just like you’d design a chart carefully, you should design a table carefully too. That means thinking about structure, formatting, styling, hierarchy—all of it.

:::

Structuring Your Data

:::

Structuring Your Data

Row grouping: Makes patterns visible - Example: Division grouping shows power distribution immediately

Column organization: Headers and spanners guide understanding - Related columns grouped, separate concerns separated

Think about structure as the skeleton of your table. When you group rows—say, by division in the NFL example—you immediately create visual clusters. The reader’s eye naturally groups things together, and they start seeing patterns without having to work hard. Same with columns. When you use headers and spanners to group related columns, you’re creating logical sections. The reader understands the organization before they ever read a single number. Good structure makes the data accessible.

Formatting & Polishing

  • Format numbers meaningfully (decimals, currency, percentages)
  • Polish with intentional styling (colors, fonts, alignment)
  • Example: Professional formatting signals “this data matters”

Formatting is about making numbers communicable. If you’re showing currency, format it as currency. If you’re showing a percentage, format it as a percentage. Don’t make your reader do the mental translation. And polishing—choosing colors, fonts, alignment—this isn’t about being pretty. It’s about clarity. When everything is aligned well, when fonts are legible, when spacing is intentional, the table becomes easier to understand. Polish signals professionalism and respect for the reader’s time.

The Art of Visual Hierarchy

Guide attention with contrast: - Color (most powerful) - Size/weight (headers vs. body) - Spacing (grouping vs. separation)

Visual hierarchy is about directing the eye. Color is the fastest channel—your brain processes color before it processes text or even shapes. Size and weight matter too. A bold header immediately signals importance. Spacing is subtler but powerful. When you group things close together, your eye sees them as related. When you separate things with space, you signal they’re different. In the NFL table, the point differential column is colored green/yellow/red—this creates instant visual hierarchy. You see the good teams and bad teams at a glance, before you read any numbers. That’s the power of good visual hierarchy.

What is gt-extras?

Extension package that adds powerful capabilities to Great Tables

Built on the foundation. Focused on storytelling.

I spent the summer building gt-extras. It was an exciting challenge because Python and R are different, and I had to rethink everything from scratch. But the core idea is simple: Great Tables gives you the foundation—the components, the structure, the basic styling. gt-extras adds the creative power-ups that let you tell stories more effectively. We’re talking about embedding visualizations inside table cells, using color as a data channel, pre-built professional themes, and more. All of it designed to make your data more accessible and more memorable.

Plotting Functions ⭐ (PRIMARY FOCUS)

“Put Small Multiples Inside Your Table Cells”

  • gt_plt_bar(), gt_plt_dumbbell(), gt_plt_winloss()
  • gt_plt_bullet(), gt_plt_sparkline()

These are the heart of gt-extras. Each one lets you embed a different type of visualization directly into a table cell. Bar charts for comparing magnitudes. Dumbbell plots for showing before/after or target vs. actual. Win/loss charts for showing streaks and records. Bullet charts for performance tracking. Sparklines for showing trends. The key insight is that when you put a visualization right next to the raw numbers, in the same cell, something magical happens. The reader gets both the precise value and the visual context. In the NFL table, the win/loss streaks show you the whole season at a glance—you can see which teams were dominant, which were inconsistent, which had late-season collapses. That’s something a raw number could never convey.

Coloring & Highlighting Functions

“Leverage Color as a Data Channel”

  • gt_color_box() - Heat map coloring
  • gt_highlight_cols() / gt_highlight_rows()
  • gt_add_divider() - Visual separation

Your brain processes color instantaneously. Before it reads words, before it processes shapes, it sees color. So why not use that? gt_color_box() creates heat maps where the color intensity represents the value. Your eye immediately sees which values are high and which are low. Highlighting functions let you draw attention strategically—maybe you want to highlight the winning team or the outliers. Dividers create visual structure without adding complexity. In the NFL table, the point differential column is colored green/yellow/red. You know at a glance who’s dominant and who’s struggling. No numbers needed—though they’re there if you want precision.

Theming Functions

  • gt_theme_538() - FiveThirtyEight style
  • gt_theme_dot_matrix() - Playful, retro aesthetic
  • (and more)

I probably spent the most time on these theming functions, and honestly it was because the styling work by Thomas Mock in the R package was just so good. Why reinvent the wheel? These themes are pre-built collections of styling choices—colors, fonts, spacing, borders—all designed to work together. One command applies them all. What took hours of CSS work becomes one line of code. The insight here is that Great Tables handles the technical details; gt-extras provides the polish and the pre-researched design choices from data journalism and other fields.

Iconing & Imaging Functions

  • gt_fa_rank_change() - Icons for trends
  • fmt_image() - Embed images in cells

Sometimes text is slower than an icon. An arrow pointing up or down communicates direction instantly. An image of a logo communicates identity faster than text. These functions let you embed both into your tables. In the NFL table, team logos replace team names. You recognize your favorite team immediately. That’s the power of visual elements.
